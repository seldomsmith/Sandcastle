# Public Transit Service Allocation Simulation: Project Charter

## 1.0 Project Overview and Vision
### 1.1 High-Level Concept
This project is a resource management and spatial planning simulation centred on the strategic allocation of municipal transit services. The player assumes the role of a transit planning authority tasked with building and managing a public transit network within an autonomously expanding urban grid. The simulation begins with a concentrated 10x10 populated urban core and a minimal starting budget of service hours. The primary objective is to strategically allocate these finite hours to maximize ridership, optimize network efficiency, and adapt to shifting demographic demands. As the player successfully captures ridership and generates revenue, their agency budget increases to unlock additional service hours. The central challenge arises because the city grows and evolves independently of the player. The pace of this urban sprawl intentionally outstrips resource generation, forcing the player to prioritize strategic expansion over blanket coverage while adapting to new urban layouts and population densities.

### 1.2 Core Design Philosophy
The core design philosophy emphasizes the tension between spatial coverage, temporal resource management, and dynamic civic growth. The central challenge requires the player to balance the high-volume, time-specific needs of peak-hour commuters against the continuous, lower-volume demands of all-purpose riders. Commuter demands necessitate massive temporary spikes in service hour allocation, while all-purpose riders require consistent baseline frequency. The game forces players to master this temporal efficiency to prevent resource waste. As the simulated city expands stochastically from a dense urban core into lower-density sprawling neighbourhoods, the player must continuously recalibrate route trajectories, stop placements, schedule frequencies, and fare structures. The cognitive experience is rooted in proactive adaptation, challenging the player to impose elegant order upon messy and unpredictable demographic data. Success in the simulation depends on maintaining operational sustainability. This requires the player to actively avoid the transit death spiral where inefficient service leads to ridership loss and subsequent resource depletion, all while capturing the maximum possible market share across an evolving environment.

### 1.3 Target Audience and Platform
The simulation is designed as a web browser game. The target audience includes casual players and puzzle enthusiasts, but it integrates robust data analytics to create a highly engaging experience. To prevent complex spatial and temporal data from alienating this audience, the system translates heavy analytics into an accessible visual format. A strict reliance on intuitive, colour-coded heatmaps and interactive graphs ensures that deep resource management remains visually approachable. Furthermore, the inclusion of flexible time controls allows for variable session pacing, accommodating both short strategic bursts and extended management play styles. The game operates as an endless sandbox where the primary goal is operational survival. To provide structural progression within this endless format, the player will unlock specific achievements as they reach population milestones, hit revenue targets, or maintain high fleet quality over time. These achievements function as secondary goals that continuously motivate the player and guide them toward mastering deeper simulation mechanics.

## 2.0 Gameplay Mechanics
### 2.1 The Primary Gameplay Loop
The core gameplay loop requires continuous strategic adjustment in response to the autonomous expansion of the city. The player controls the flow of time by pausing, running at standard speed, or using acceleration multipliers (2x, 5x, and 10x). The interaction follows a distinct operational sequence of observation, planning, execution, and evaluation. During the observation phase, the player analyses dashboard metrics and visual heatmaps to identify emerging demographic hotspots or areas of declining ridership. In the planning phase, the player pauses the simulation to construct routes, assign bus stops, and adjust frequency schedules. The player then resumes time to execute these changes and evaluates the resulting operational impact on their service hour budget, overall ridership, and revenue generation. If the player fails to engage with this loop as the city expands, the existing transit network will rapidly become obsolete. This inaction leads to an increase in non-riders, stagnating revenue, and the eventual transit death spiral of the network.

### 2.1.1 Simulation State and Action Queuing
The temporal controls require a strict separation of state within the Redux architecture. When the player pauses the simulation, the backend ticking engine halts, pausing the accumulation of revenue, fleet degradation, and urban expansion. However, the user interface remains fully active. Any modifications made by the player, such as drawing new routes or placing bus stops, are temporarily stored in a staging environment rather than immediately impacting the active city data. When the player resumes the simulation, a commit function fires, instantly merging the staging data into the active simulation state. This logic ensures that complex network overhauls process cleanly without calculating partial trip data while the player is mid-build.

### 2.1.2 Simulation Initialization and the Day 0 State
To prevent immediate operational failure, the sandbox initializes in a permanently paused "Day 0" state. Upon loading a new city, the 10x10 core is fully populated, but the autonomous clock remains frozen. This provides the player with unlimited time to analyze the initial heatmap, design their foundational transit routes, allocate their starting 48 service hours, and finalize their schedule. The engine does not begin calculating the rolling 24-hour survival threshold until the player manually unpauses the simulation and the clock officially rolls over to Day 1.

### 2.2 Route Construction and Infrastructure Placement
Players construct transit routes using a precise click and drag interface. The transit lines snap to the grid vertices, run along the borders connecting individual grid squares, and strictly avoid cutting directly through the centre of the lots. This physical framework allows multiple distinct routes to share corridors and intersections, enabling functional passenger transfer hubs. Bus stop placement is a discrete action separate from route drawing. This separation empowers players to design specific transit typologies, ranging from localized neighbourhood routes with frequent stops to rapid express routes that bypass certain areas to optimize travel times. Every placed bus stop consumes a flat portion of the service hour budget to account for required vehicle dwell times. Players can edit existing routes endlessly to adapt to the expanding city. Modifying a route incurs a temporary ridership penalty. This mechanic simulates passenger confusion, introduces an adjustment period, and causes an immediate ridership drop that regenerates gradually over several in-game days as the demographic adapts to the new layout.

### 2.2.1 Route Vector Mapping Algorithm
The click and drag interface relies on a strict edge-mapping algorithm to prevent illegal physical placements. The 100x100 grid comprises horizontal and vertical edges connecting individual vertices. When the player clicks a starting vertex and drags their cursor, the algorithm calculates the shortest path along these predefined edges to the current cursor position, strictly preventing diagonal rendering across the centre of the lot. The resulting route is saved to the data structure as a sequential array of specific vertex coordinates.

### 2.2.2 Editing Penalty Application and Recovery
The temporary ridership penalty applied to modified routes utilizes a timestamped mathematical modifier. If a player edits an established route array, the system applies a 0.5 multiplier to the localized catchment data linked to that route, instantly reducing potential ridership by 50 percent. The system logs the exact in-game hour the modification occurred. A dedicated recovery function executes daily, incrementing the modifier by 0.1 for every 24 in-game hours that pass. This gradually restores the catchment potential back to the standard 1.0 baseline over a period of five simulated days, accurately modelling the demographic adjustment period.

### 2.2.3 Infrastructure Removal and Modification
Players can selectively delete or relocate existing bus stops using the Transit Infrastructure menu. Removing a bus stop instantly reclaims the one-minute service hour penalty associated with that specific node. However, this action triggers a localized ridership penalty identical to the route editing mechanic described in Section 2.2.2. The sudden loss of infrastructure forces the underlying demographic to seek alternative transit options, causing an immediate dip in localized capture rates. This penalty regenerates gradually over five simulated days as the population adapts to the modified network geometry.

### 2.3 Spatial Catchment and Passenger Demand Models
Transit networks capture ridership exclusively within the immediate vicinity of physical bus stops. The system utilizes a weighted half-life decay formula to calculate potential ridership based on the proximity of a grid square to the nearest stop. This catchment area extends outward in a radius of two to four squares. Squares immediately adjacent to a stop yield the maximum potential ridership, while the capture rate decreases progressively. The fourth square outward retains only 10 percent of the base volume compared to an immediately adjacent square. Furthermore, the origin and destination logic relies on a gravity model to simulate travel demand. This mathematical system determines travel destinations by weighing the physical distance of the journey, the required transit time, and the inherent attractiveness of high-density zones. Dense urban cores naturally draw a significantly higher volume of trips than peripheral suburbs. Trips are successfully completed, and revenue is generated, only if the established transit network provides a contiguous physical path between the origin and destination points. This connection can occur via a single direct route or through multiple intersecting lines that facilitate passenger transfers.

### 2.3.1 Network Graph Construction and Pathfinding
To verify connectivity for the gravity model, the system must abstract the physical transit routes into a mathematical directed graph. Within this backend structure, every physical bus stop functions as a graph node, and the specific route segments connecting them serve as the graph edges. When a batch of trips attempts to travel from an origin square to a destination square, the engine utilizes a pathfinding algorithm, such as Dijkstra's algorithm, to search this directed graph. The algorithm checks if an uninterrupted sequence of edges connects the origin node to the destination node. If a valid path exists, the trip batch resolves successfully.

### 2.4 Temporal Scheduling and Resource Allocation
The player manages a finite bank of service hours representing the operational budget of the transit agency. After constructing a route, the player utilizes an interactive line graph to dictate service frequency throughout the simulated day. The x-axis represents the time of day, and the y-axis represents service frequency. As the player manipulates the frequency curve, the interface provides immediate feedback by calculating the resulting service hour cost, updating the overall budget tracker, and displaying the exact number of physical vehicles required. To successfully capture the different rider typologies, players must allocate high frequency spikes during narrow commuter peaks and maintain a steady, lower baseline frequency during non-peak periods for all-purpose riders. The system enforces structural constraints on these schedules, requiring a minimum baseline frequency to keep a route active, preventing service drops to absolute zero, and imposing a maximum cap based on the total available fleet capacity. This dynamic scheduling mechanism forces the player to conserve their budget while precisely matching vehicle deployment to temporal demographic demands.

### 2.4.1 Frequency Curve Serialization
The visual line graph requires serialization to interact with the service hour logic. The x-axis of the graph is divided into 24 discrete segments representing each hour of the simulated day. As the player drags the continuous frequency line, the user interface converts the visual curve into an array containing 24 integer values. Each integer represents the scheduled frequency for that specific hour. The TypeScript engine iterates through this daily array, multiplying the frequency integers by the baseline route duration calculated in Section 5.1. This serialization process ensures that fluid visual inputs generate precise mathematical deductions from the service hour budget.

### 2.5 Game Over States and Operational Failure
The endless sandbox format requires a defined failure state to maintain strategic tension. This failure state is tied intrinsically to the transit death spiral and overall public utility metrics. The simulation ends if total active ridership falls below a critical threshold, initially set at 10 percent of the total simulated city population. The main dashboard prominently displays this key performance indicator, tracking total population alongside total ridership, which is further segmented into commuter and all-purpose categories. If the player fails to adapt to the autonomous urban expansion or allocates resources inefficiently, the resulting drop in spatial capture will push the network below this threshold, triggering a game over state.

## 3.0 Simulation Dynamics
### 3.1 Autonomous Urban Expansion and Grid Densification
The simulated city expands autonomously from an initial concentrated 10x10 core into the larger 100x100 grid. This growth occurs at a constant mathematical frequency, with exactly one new populated grid square appearing in a stochastic location every in-game hour. This steady addition creates a creeping mechanical pressure, as the continual increase in absolute city population constantly raises the volume of passengers required to maintain the 10 percent survival threshold. As new neighbourhoods emerge on the periphery, the central core simultaneously expands its footprint and increases its localized population density.

The visual environment reflecting this growth is highly elegant and minimalist. By default, the simulation presents a clean white canvas overlaid with visible but subtle half-grey grid lines. This uncluttered baseline ensures that the player can focus purely on route construction, spatial planning, and strategic oversight without visual distraction. Transit lines utilize bold, distinct colours to create a sharp contrast against this minimalist background, ensuring immediate visual clarity as the network expands. The grid lines serve a functional purpose by representing the strict structural boundaries of the city lots. They render in a soft, non-intrusive grey to provide a reliable sense of scale and physical distance. To manage visual complexity as the simulated city grows to its maximum capacity, the design prioritizes negative space and allows the player to selectively toggle layers. This ensures that the primary focus remains securely on critical transit infrastructure, operational metrics, and network intersections.

### 3.1.1 Algorithmic Grid Expansion Engine
The backend mechanism driving the physical expansion relies on a recurring TypeScript function tied directly to the in-game clock state. Every time the simulation advances by exactly 60 in-game minutes, a grid expansion payload is dispatched. To ensure the city mimics organic urban sprawl rather than generating disconnected grid coordinates, the engine utilizes a weighted adjacency algorithm. The system evaluates the master array of 10,000 squares, filters for unpopulated squares that immediately border an actively populated square, and applies a weighted random selection. Squares situated closer to the densified core possess a slightly higher probability of selection, ensuring the city grows as a contiguous mass with realistic perimeter sprawl.

### 3.1.2 Demographic Data Generation
Upon the hourly selection of a new grid square, the system initializes localized data attributes within the Redux store. The engine calculates the Euclidean distance between the newly activated square and the geographic centre of the original 10x10 core. This distance variable acts as a modifier to determine the baseline population density and the specific demographic typology ratio of the new lot. Squares generated closer to the core receive a higher minimum population and a demographic ratio skewed toward all-purpose riders. Squares generated further outward receive a lower absolute population and a ratio heavily weighted toward peak-hour commuters, accurately simulating the emergence of suburban commuter belts.

### 3.1.3 Dynamic Threshold Calculation
The creeping mechanical pressure is executed via a continuous state evaluation within the Redux architecture. As each new square generates its population variables, the global city population variable increments automatically. The backend engine continuously executes a strict formula calculating 10 percent of this total global population. This resulting integer represents the absolute minimum daily ridership required for operational survival. To evaluate player success fairly, the system compares this dynamic threshold against a rolling 24-hour average of successfully completed transit trips. If the rolling average of completed trips falls below the dynamically increasing survival integer, the engine initiates the operational failure sequence.

### 3.1.4 Algorithmic Densification and Growth Curves
Existing populated squares grow their internal populations through a daily stochastic growth tick. Every 24 in-game hours, a backend function iterates through all active squares and applies a compounded growth formula. The engine utilizes a baseline daily growth rate of 3 percent. This baseline is modified by a random variable generation and a proximity multiplier weighted toward the geographic centre of the city. This organic mathematical model ensures that a specific square will roughly double its total population every 24 in-game days until it reaches a maximum defined density cap. This continuous background densification ensures the player must constantly upgrade route frequencies to manage increasing localized demand.

### 3.2 Demographic Typologies and Trip Generation
Grid squares possess dynamic demographic profiles that evolve over time. Increased density within the expanding urban core correlates with a higher volume of total riders and a greater proportion of all-purpose riders. The visual representation reflects these demographic shifts. The highly populated urban centre renders as a deep purple to indicate a dense mixture of commuter and all-purpose demand. This colour gradient fades outward toward white in the lower density suburbs, featuring subtle tinges of red, purple, or blue to denote the specific localized demographic makeup.

### 3.2.1 Aggregate Demand Batching
To maintain optimal browser performance, the simulation avoids individual agent-based modelling. Instead, the backend utilizes aggregate demand batching. Each grid square possesses a daily trip budget based on its absolute population and demographic ratio. For example, a square with 1,000 residents generates a specific mathematical volume of potential daily trips. The system treats these trips as a fluid volume, releasing them into an active demand pool at specific temporal intervals, which the gravity model then attempts to resolve against the active transit network.

### 3.2.2 Temporal Release Functions
The release of this trip volume relies on distinct temporal probability functions. Commuter populations utilize a bidirectional bell curve. A large percentage of their daily outbound trip budget is released within the morning peak window, and the corresponding inbound return trips are released during the evening peak. Conversely, the all-purpose demographic utilizes a continuous, flattened probability distribution. Their daily trip budget releases at a steady, low-volume rate across the entire 24-hour cycle, requiring the player to maintain consistent baseline service frequencies to capture this demographic successfully.

### 3.2.3 Bidirectional Trip Resolution
The system must mathematically link origins and destinations to ensure realistic travel patterns. When the engine generates morning commuter trips targeting a specific high-density commercial square, the Redux store temporarily caches that successful spatial link. During the evening peak, the engine reverses this exact geographical relationship. The commercial destination becomes the new origin, and the original residential square becomes the targeted destination. This ensures that riders who successfully travel to a central hub must utilize the transit network to return, effectively doubling the revenue potential of a well-planned route while punishing networks lacking bidirectional capacity.

### 3.3 Origin-Destination Logic and Network Connectivity
As the physical footprint of the city evolves, the travel demands of the underlying population shift accordingly. Riders generate trips based on a weighted probability model that naturally draws them toward newly densified zones and the central urban core. The player must continuously monitor these organic demographic shifts and adjust their transit network to ensure functional connectivity between emerging residential zones and high-density hubs.

### 3.4 Fleet Degradation and Maintenance
Transit vehicles experience progressive wear and tear strictly based on the accumulation of active service hours. As the aggregate fleet quality degrades without financial investment, vehicles suffer mechanical slowdowns. This introduces a mathematical time penalty to every active route, extending travel durations and demanding additional service hours to maintain established frequencies. The player must actively monitor this quality metric and allocate treasury capital to restore the fleet, balancing the cost of necessary maintenance against the desire for network expansion.

### 3.4.1 Degradation Mathematics and Time Penalties
The backend engine tracks the continual consumption of service hours. For every 24 service hours actively executed by the transit network, the global fleet quality metric decreases by exactly 1 percent. This degradation translates directly into an operational penalty. For every 1 percent drop in fleet quality below maximum capacity, a corresponding 1 percent time penalty is applied to the baseline travel speed of all active routes. For example, a fleet operating at 80 percent quality will suffer a 20 percent penalty, increasing the baseline traversal speed from six in-game minutes per square to 7.2 in-game minutes. This mathematically demands a larger allocation from the player's finite pool of service hours to maintain the exact same frequency schedule.

### 3.4.2 The Interactive Maintenance Slider
The player interacts with the maintenance system through a dedicated module within the Fleet and Finance menu. This interface features a dynamic slider representing the current fleet quality on a 0 to 100 percent scale. As the fleet degrades, the visual slider physically moves downward along the track. The player dictates maintenance spending by dragging the slider back upward toward 100 percent, or to any intermediate point they deem strategically appropriate.

### 3.4.3 The Economic Cost of Repair
The user interface provides immediate financial feedback during the maintenance process. As the player drags the quality slider upward, the menu dynamically displays the exact capital required to execute the repair. The cost is calculated at a fixed flat rate of $150 for every 1 percent of quality restored. When the player releases the slider and confirms the action, the TypeScript engine instantly deducts the total calculated cost from the treasury and resets the fleet quality metric, immediately removing the corresponding time penalties from the active network.

## 4.0 Visual Interface and User Experience
### 4.1 Aesthetic Direction and Grid Visualization
The visual environment is highly elegant and minimalist. By default, the simulation presents a clean white canvas overlaid with visible but subtle half-grey grid lines. This uncluttered baseline ensures that the player can focus purely on route construction, spatial planning, and strategic oversight without visual distraction. Transit lines utilize bold, distinct colours to create a sharp contrast against this minimalist background, ensuring immediate visual clarity as the network expands. The grid lines serve a functional purpose by representing the strict structural boundaries of the city lots. They render in a soft, non-intrusive grey to provide a reliable sense of scale and physical distance. To manage visual complexity as the simulated city grows to its maximum capacity, the design prioritizes negative space and allows the player to selectively toggle layers. This ensures that the primary focus remains securely on critical transit infrastructure, operational metrics, and network intersections.

### 4.1.1 Canvas and Base Grid Rendering Engine
The foundational visual layer operates on a WebGL canvas rendered via PixiJS. To maintain maximum performance and aesthetic minimalism, the baseline application generates a static absolute white background layer mapped to the hex code `#FFFFFF`. Overlaid upon this background is the grid structure. The engine draws the grid lines using a precise half-grey hex value of `#CCCCCC`. This soft contrast ensures the lines remain highly visible for accurate route snapping while preventing them from visually overpowering the demographic data or the vibrant transit corridors.

### 4.2 Demographic Heatmaps and Colour Theory
The player can toggle the demographic heatmap on or off using map options located within the main user interface dock but it will be on by default. This toggle function allows the player to alternate between diagnosing demographic demand and viewing a clean canvas for precise route management. When activated, the grid visualizes underlying population data using specific colour gradients. The system maps distinct demographics to primary colours, where red represents concentrations of peak-hour commuters and blue indicates all-purpose riders. Purple naturally occurs where these two demographics overlap. Furthermore, colour saturation directly correlates with absolute population volume. Deep purple represents dense urban cores with a heavy mix of commuter and all-purpose demand. This colour fades outward toward a pale white in the lower density suburbs. These peripheral areas display subtle tinges of red, blue, or purple to indicate their localized demographic ratio, while the reduced colour saturation visually communicates the lower overall transit capacity required.

### 4.2.1 Demographic Hue Interpolation
The dynamic colour shifting on the grid relies on a direct mathematical relationship between the localized demographic ratio and the Red, Green, and Blue (RGB) colour channels. The engine maps the peak-hour commuter demographic strictly to the red channel and the all-purpose demographic strictly to the blue channel. The green channel remains permanently set to zero.

When the Redux store dispatches a square update, a TypeScript function calculates the exact hue by evaluating the ratio of the two demographics. If a square possesses 1,000 total citizens comprising 750 commuters and 250 all-purpose riders, the engine assigns an RGB value of `rgb(191, 0, 64)`. This mathematical interpolation ensures a seamless, organic visual gradient. A purely commuter square renders as absolute red `rgb(255, 0, 0)`, a purely all-purpose square renders as absolute blue `rgb(0, 0, 255)`, and a perfectly balanced square renders as absolute purple `rgb(127, 0, 127)`.

### 4.2.2 Population Density Opacity Scaling
Colour saturation communicates absolute population density through the manipulation of the Alpha transparency channel in an RGBA rendering model. The engine utilizes the pure white `#FFFFFF` background canvas to create the fading visual effect. The TypeScript formula calculates the current total population of a square against the maximum defined capacity of a high-density urban block.

The resulting percentage dictates the Alpha value. A square operating at 100 percent capacity receives an Alpha value of `1.0`, rendering the demographic hue as deep, fully saturated, and opaque. A sprawling suburban square operating at only 20 percent capacity receives an Alpha value of `0.20`. Because the colour is rendered at 20 percent opacity over a pure white background, it visually presents as a pale, washed-out pastel. This approach guarantees that high-demand urban cores visually dominate the screen as deep purple hotspots, naturally drawing the player's strategic attention.

### 4.2.3 Master Coverage Overlay
To evaluate macro-level network efficiency, the player can activate a Master Coverage Overlay from the map options menu located on the main dock. Activating this toggle replaces the standard demographic heatmap with a binary visual filter. Grid squares currently falling within the active catchment area of any operational bus stop render in a soft green, while entirely unserved populated squares render in a stark, contrasting grey. This visualization allows the player to instantly identify transit deserts, assess overall municipal coverage, and plan future network expansion priorities without needing to parse complex demographic ratios.

### 4.3 The Primary Dashboard and Menu System
The user interface relies on a permanent dock located at the bottom edge of the screen. This dock anchors the core temporal controls, allowing the player to instantly pause, play, or accelerate the simulation at any moment. Several distinct management menus can be opened from this central hub. These include Map Options for toggling demographic heatmaps, Route Management for building and editing transit lines, and a Key Performance Indicators panel tracking service hours, ridership, and revenue. Additionally, the dock houses a Fleet and Finance menu where the player can adjust fare pricing, allocate maintenance budgets, and monitor overall fleet quality. A separate Progression tab tracks unlocked achievements and population milestones to provide structural goals. All opened menus float dynamically over the grid canvas. The interface gives the player the ability to move, pin, or close these modules as needed. This flexible layout allows players to customize their workspace while maintaining immediate access to critical transit data.

### 4.3.1 Permanent Dock Metrics
To ensure immediate operational awareness, the bottom dock permanently displays four critical data points at all times without requiring the player to open any secondary menus. These constant visual metrics include the total absolute ridership, ridership expressed as a percentage of the total simulated population, the current number of available service hours, and the aggregate fleet quality displayed as a percentage. This permanent visibility allows the player to continuously monitor their distance from the 10 percent operational survival threshold while actively manipulating the map.

### 4.3.2 Progression and Milestones
The Progression tab tracks player milestones within the endless sandbox format. To maintain the strict balance of the in-game economy, unlocking these achievements does not grant financial bonuses or additional service hours. Instead, they serve purely as structural goals to guide the player through network expansion. Specific unlockable milestones include reaching absolute ridership targets such as 10,000 or 100,000 completed trips, achieving network density goals like serving 30 percent or 50 percent of the total population, and hitting operational scaling targets such as successfully managing 10, 20, or 30 distinct bus routes.

### 4.3.3 Session Persistence and Data Serialization
Because the game runs entirely within a web browser, it utilizes the LocalStorage application programming interface to manage session persistence. The backend automatically serializes the entire Redux state string and saves it to LocalStorage at the stroke of midnight every in-game day. This prevents total data loss in the event of an accidental browser closure. Furthermore, the permanent dock includes a dedicated manual save button. This allows the player to force a state save immediately before attempting a massive, risky network overhaul, ensuring they can revert their progress if their new infrastructure fails to capture the required demographic volume.

### 4.4 Route Customization and Interactive Graphs
Players manage transit lines through a dedicated route menu accessed from the permanent dock. Opening this menu reveals a comprehensive list of all active bus routes. Clicking a specific route opens a separate, dynamic popup window dedicated entirely to that line. This interface features an interactive graph where the player drags a line up and down to adjust service frequency throughout the simulated day. To assist in strategic planning, the graph overlays anticipated demographic demand curves, allowing players to visually match their scheduled service spikes directly against expected rider volumes. Beyond scheduling, this popup serves as the central customization hub where players can rename routes and utilize an integrated colour wheel to assign distinct visual identities. The system dynamically calculates and displays localized performance metrics, including specific ridership capture, localized non-riders, and the total number of service hours consumed. This calculation instantly updates the number of physical buses allocated to the route, reinforcing that spatial and temporal design remains the core determinant of resource consumption.

### 4.4.1 Route-Specific Catchment Visualization
When a player clicks on a specific route within the Route Management menu, the visual engine isolates that transit line to assist with micro-level planning. The primary demographic heatmap fades to a low opacity, reducing overall visual clutter. The engine then renders concentric, semi-transparent bubbles radiating outward from every bus stop along the selected route. These bubbles utilize a gradient opacity to visually represent the underlying half-life spatial decay formula. The squares immediately adjacent to the stop render brightly, and the visual intensity decreases progressively across the four-square catchment radius. This provides the player with immediate, intuitive feedback regarding exactly which demographic squares that specific route services.

### 4.5 Physical Entity Abstraction
To maintain the elegant and minimalist aesthetic, the simulation operates entirely as an abstract mathematical layer. The visual interface does not render individual, moving physical vehicles along the transit corridors. Instead, the bold, colour-coded transit lines and the dynamic demographic heatmaps represent the entire operational flow. This abstraction ensures the user interface remains uncluttered and focuses the player's attention strictly on macroscopic data analysis, temporal scheduling, and strategic spatial planning.

### 4.5.1 Abstract Route Rendering System
Because the system abstracts physical vehicles, the visual transit lines must convey absolute clarity. The route drawing function generates distinct vector lines that sit atop the highest rendering layer (the z-index). The engine assigns route colours dynamically from a predefined, highly saturated hex palette designed specifically to contrast against the red, blue, and purple demographic heatmaps. This palette excludes shades of red or blue to prevent visual confusion. Permitted default hex values include neon green (`#00FF00`), bright yellow (`#FFFF00`), and vivid orange (`#FF9800`). The line drawing mechanism snaps strictly to the grid vertices and borders, calculating smooth corner transitions to prevent jagged visual overlaps when multiple routes share a single transit corridor.

### 4.6 Camera Navigation and Viewport Controls
To facilitate precise route planning across a massive 10,000-square grid, the player requires advanced camera manipulation tools. The visual engine must support dynamic scaling, 3D plane pitching, and full orbital rotation. Transitioning from a strictly top-down 2D view to a manipulatable 2.5D perspective requires updating the WebGL canvas to support perspective projection matrices. This allows the flat grid to skew and rotate realistically within a 3D spatial environment while retaining its minimalist aesthetic.

### 4.6.1 Viewport Zoom and Spatial Scaling
The player can smoothly scale the map to transition between a macroscopic city view and a fine-tuned neighbourhood perspective. The backend handles this through mouse wheel event listeners. Scrolling the mouse wheel adjusts the camera distance variable along the Z-axis. To ensure the player maintains visual context, the TypeScript engine must enforce strict minimum and maximum zoom clamps. The minimum clamp prevents the camera from clipping through the grid floor, and the maximum clamp restricts the camera from zooming out beyond the absolute borders of the 100x100 playable area.

### 4.6.2 3D Pitch and Map Rotation Logic
The player utilizes the right mouse button to manipulate the physical orientation of the grid. This requires an event listener tracking mouse movement while the right button is depressed. The engine separates this input into two distinct variables.
Dragging the mouse horizontally calculates a delta X value. The engine maps this value to the camera yaw, allowing the user to seamlessly rotate the map 360 degrees to shift their north, south, east, and west orientations.
Dragging the mouse vertically calculates a delta Y value. The engine maps this value to the camera pitch, allowing the user to tilt the flat grid into a 3D perspective. To maintain immersion and prevent the player from viewing the empty space beneath the rendering plane, the engine must apply a strict mathematical clamp to the polar angle. This polar clamp restricts the pitch between 0 degrees for a perfect top-down view and approximately 60 degrees for an angled perspective.

### 4.6.3 Compass Interface and Orientation Reset
To orient the player during complex camera rotations, the user interface includes a minimalist compass anchored to the corner of the screen. This compass operates as a standalone React component layered over the WebGL canvas. It reads the current rotation angle directly from the camera state data and dynamically updates its visual needle to indicate true north. Furthermore, the compass functions as an interactive reset button. An event listener attached to the left mouse button detects when the player clicks the compass icon. Upon clicking, a function triggers a smooth camera interpolation, automatically resetting the camera yaw to 0 degrees and orienting the grid perfectly north.

### 4.7 Transit Infrastructure Construction Menu
The player initiates all physical network expansion through a dedicated Transit Infrastructure tab accessed via a prominent button on the permanent dock. Opening this tab reveals a specialized floating menu containing three primary action buttons: Create Route, Place Bus Stop, and Modify Network. Placing a bus stop requires zero financial capital, as the mechanical cost is strictly represented by the service hour time penalty detailed in Section 5.1. When the player clicks the Place Bus Stop button, the interface enters a placement mode. The user cursor dynamically snaps to the nearest grid vertex. Bus stops can only be placed precisely at the intersections of the grid lines, meaning a single stop naturally touches exactly four distinct grid squares. Clicking the left mouse button locks the infrastructure into place.

### 4.7.1 Infrastructure Visual Rendering
To maintain the minimalist aesthetic, the physical infrastructure utilizes clean geometric shapes. Routes render as solid coloured lines tracing the grid borders. A bus stop renders as a distinct circle centred perfectly on the specified grid intersection. If the stop services a single route, the circle renders in the exact solid colour of that specific line. If the player builds an intersection where multiple distinct routes share the same physical stop, the stop functions as a transfer hub. In this scenario, the circle automatically renders as a solid black dot. This high-contrast visual cue immediately signals the presence of a functional transfer point to the player without requiring them to open additional data menus.

### 4.7.2 Transfer Node Assignment Logic
When a player places a bus stop directly onto an intersection containing multiple distinct route lines, the physical infrastructure is not automatically shared. Instead, placing the stop triggers a localized menu popup detailing every route vector occupying that specific vertex. The player utilizes checkboxes to manually assign the bus stop to specific lines. The player can assign the stop to a single route, or they can assign it to multiple routes simultaneously. If multiple routes are selected, the backend engine immediately mathematically links the arrays and updates the visual rendering of the bus stop to a solid black circle, confirming its new status as an active transfer node for the gravity model.

### 4.8 User Onboarding and Interactive Tutorial
Given the complexity of the spatial and temporal mechanics, the game will feature a formalized interactive tutorial for new players. Rather than relying on passive tooltips, this step-by-step onboarding sequence guides the user through the core gameplay loop within a highly controlled instance of the initial 10x10 grid. The tutorial requires the player to physically execute essential actions, including constructing their first transit route, placing functional bus stops, and manipulating the interactive frequency graph to match a predetermined demographic peak. Once the player successfully executes these foundational mechanics, the game automatically transitions into the standard, autonomously expanding sandbox environment.

## 5.0 Game Logic and Mathematical Models
### 5.1 Service Hour Consumption and Route Calculations
The total service hours consumed by a route depend on its physical length, its assigned frequency schedule, and a baseline operational overhead. The simulation relies on a strict mathematical anchor for vehicle speed. A standard, fully maintained bus requires 60 in-game minutes to traverse 10 grid blocks, establishing a baseline speed of six in-game minutes per square. 

The system calculates total trip duration by multiplying the route length by this baseline speed. This base duration is then modified by physical network factors. Navigating corners along the grid vertices introduces a slight time penalty to encourage direct corridors. Furthermore, every placed bus stop introduces a penalty of exactly one in-game minute to account for vehicle deceleration, passenger boarding, and vehicle acceleration. 

The engine uses this finalized trip duration to determine physical resource costs. For example, a route of 10 blocks requires a return trip of 20 blocks, equating to a baseline duration of 120 in-game minutes. To maintain a frequency of one arrival every 60 minutes, the route requires two physical buses. If the player increases the frequency to every 15 minutes during a commuter peak, the requirement scales proportionally to eight buses. This final calculation determines the aggregate service hours consumed over the simulated 24-hour cycle. Finally, every active route incurs a minimum baseline overhead cost to simulate depot travel and administrative resources.

### 5.2 The Half-Life Spatial Decay Formula
The spatial catchment area of a bus stop extends outward in a radius of two to four grid squares. The system uses a spatial decay formula to calculate potential ridership capture based on realistic pedestrian walking paths. Distance is measured using strict grid traversal rather than a direct geometric line. Squares immediately adjacent to a bus stop yield the highest potential ridership, while the capture rate decreases progressively as distance increases. The fourth square outward retains approximately 10 percent of the capture potential compared to an immediately adjacent square. This calculated percentage acts as a multiplier applied to the absolute population of the specific grid lot. Consequently, a high-density zone located three blocks away may generate a higher absolute volume of riders than a low-density zone situated directly next to the stop. In scenarios where the catchment areas of multiple bus stops overlap, the simulation allocates the population demand to the closest stop, prevents the duplication of potential riders, and ensures accurate data reporting.

### 5.2.1 Algorithmic Catchment Processing
The backend calculation of the catchment area requires a precise algorithmic sweep whenever a player places a new bus stop. Because the stop sits perfectly on a grid intersection, it immediately borders four distinct grid squares. When the placement action is confirmed, the TypeScript engine triggers a localized Breadth-First Search algorithm restricted to a maximum radius of four grid squares.

The algorithm assigns a 100 percent capture modifier to the four base squares immediately touching the intersection. As the search algorithm iterates outward to the second, third, and fourth rings of squares, it applies the progressively shrinking half-life decay multiplier to the underlying demographic data. The engine then updates the localized Redux store variables for those specific squares, mathematically linking their generated trip volume directly to that specific bus stop node. To handle overlapping catchment zones efficiently, the algorithm always checks the existing state of a square. If a square is already linked to an older bus stop, the engine compares the grid distance. The square will automatically reassign its trip volume to the newly built stop only if the new node provides a mathematically shorter walking distance for the underlying population.

### 5.3 The Gravity Model for Destination Selection
Riders generate trips based on a gravity model. This mathematical system determines travel destinations by weighing the distance between origin points and the attractiveness of high-density zones. Within this equation, destination density exerts a stronger mathematical pull than physical distance, simulating the reality that riders will tolerate longer travel times to reach dense urban cores and major employment hubs. Trips have varying lengths, and a journey is only completed if the player's transit network physically connects the origin square, the destination square, and any required transfer points. To accurately model passenger friction, the system imposes a time-based transfer penalty whenever a journey requires moving between separate transit lines. A generated trip will be abandoned, categorized as uncaptured demand, and fail to generate revenue if the transit network lacks connectivity, if the required transit time significantly exceeds the direct physical distance, or if the route demands an excessive number of transfers.

### 5.3.1 Population Proportionality and Destination Gravity
The gravity model utilizes a direct, proportional mathematical relationship to determine destination attractiveness. The base probability of a grid square being selected as a travel destination scales exactly 1:1 with its absolute population. For example, a dense urban hub possessing 1,000 citizens exerts exactly 100 times more mathematical pull on the surrounding network than a newly generated suburban lot containing 10 citizens. This calculation dictates that the vast majority of generated trips will naturally attempt to route inward toward the oldest, most densified sectors of the 100x100 grid.

### 5.3.2 Transfer Penalty Constraints
While the network graph allows for multi-route connectivity, the engine applies mathematical friction to prevent the exploitation of fragmented routes. The system applies a flat penalty of exactly five in-game minutes to the total transit duration every time a generated trip requires a passenger to transfer from one distinct route line to another. This 5-minute penalty ensures that direct routes remain significantly more efficient and capture a higher percentage of the demographic demand than journeys requiring multiple connections.

### 5.3.3 Walking Speed and Trip Abandonment
To determine if a generated trip successfully translates into captured ridership, the engine evaluates the transit travel time against a baseline pedestrian walking speed. The simulation sets the pedestrian walking speed at a constant 15 in-game minutes per grid square. When a trip is generated, the backend calculates the theoretical direct walking time between the origin and destination. It then calculates the total transit time, which encompasses the physical route length, the one-minute penalty for every traversed bus stop, and any accumulated five-minute transfer penalties. If the calculated total transit time exceeds the theoretical direct walking time, the virtual demographic abandons the transit network. These failed trips register immediately on the main dashboard as uncaptured demand, negatively impacting the operational survival threshold.

### 5.4 Fare Elasticity and Revenue Generation
The player establishes a single, network-wide fare price through the Fleet and Finance menu. The simulation calculates total revenue by applying a fare elasticity model to the two distinct rider demographics against a default baseline price. All-purpose riders demonstrate a highly inelastic demand with an elasticity coefficient of approximately 0.2, indicating that price changes have a minimal impact on their likelihood to ride. Commuters exhibit a more elastic demand with a coefficient of approximately 0.3, making their ridership levels more sensitive to fare adjustments. Increasing the fare above the baseline triggers a proportional drop in ridership based on these coefficients. Decreasing the fare stimulates passenger volume but reduces the capital earned per completed trip. To assist with strategic planning, the interface provides a dynamic projection tool that previews estimated shifts in demographic capture and revenue before the player confirms a price change. Optimizing this pricing structure is critical, as reaching specific revenue thresholds directly unlocks the additional service hours required to fund network expansion.

### 5.5 Time Scaling and Temporal Mapping
The simulation relies on a strict mathematical conversion between real time and in-game time. At standard simulation speed, one complete 24-hour in-game day equals exactly three minutes of real time. Players can accelerate time up to a 10x multiplier, reducing a full simulated day to 18 seconds, though this introduces the strategic risk of missing critical demographic shifts. The daily demographic logic dictates that morning commuter peaks occur between 07:00 and 09:00, and evening commuter peaks occur between 15:00 and 17:00 in-game time. These peaks do not function as flat blocks of demand. Instead, they operate on a bell curve, featuring a gradual increase, an absolute maximum volume at the centre of the window, and a gradual decrease. The interactive frequency graph maps directly to these temporal windows, requiring the player to perfectly align their service spikes with these specific mathematical intervals to efficiently capture peak ridership.

### 5.6 The Economic Conversion Cycle and Fleet Aging
The financial economy bridges the gap between passenger volume and actionable resources. Revenue functions as an accumulated currency rather than a passive milestone tracker. Fares collected from successful trips populate the agency treasury. The player must manually allocate this capital, choosing between purchasing permanent additions to their maximum service hour budget or funding fleet maintenance. Fleet condition is tracked via a metric representing the average age of the vehicles. As the fleet ages, vehicles become exponentially more expensive to maintain and operationally slower. Slower buses require a greater volume of service hours to complete the exact same physical route. This forces a constant strategic choice between expanding network capacity and maintaining the efficiency of the existing infrastructure.

### 5.7 Economic Variables and Progression Mathematics
The game economy relies on strict mathematical variables to balance operational difficulty against the pace of urban expansion. The simulation initializes with the 10x10 urban core holding an aggregate population of approximately 10,000 citizens. Based on the 10 percent survival threshold, the player must capture a minimum of 1,000 daily trips to prevent a game over state.

The default baseline fare is set at $3.00 per completed trip. The player can adjust this fare via the Fleet and Finance menu using a slider moving in $0.05 increments. These adjustments directly interact with the elasticity coefficients detailed in Section 5.4. While physically drawing transit paths on the grid incurs no cost, allocating the temporal schedule requires service hours. The player begins the simulation with a free foundational budget of 48 service hours.

To expand the network beyond this initial capacity, the player must utilize their accumulated treasury revenue to purchase additional, permanent service hours. The capital cost of one permanent service hour is fixed at $500. For example, adding sufficient capacity to run one additional physical vehicle for a full 24-hour cycle requires 24 service hours, representing a capital investment of $12,000. This pricing structure dictates that a highly optimized network capturing a large market share will generate sufficient daily capital to outpace the autonomous city expansion, while an inefficient network operating near the survival threshold will lack the funding required to grow.

### 5.7.1 State Management for Financial Data
The economic system requires a dedicated slice within the Redux state management architecture. This financial store will track the current treasury balance, the active fare price, the total pool of purchased service hours, and the aggregate fleet degradation metric. By centralizing these variables, the system ensures that the React user interface, the mathematical simulation engine, and the visual dashboard remain perfectly synchronized without data conflicts.

### 5.7.2 Revenue Generation Algorithm
Revenue generation operates through a continuous calculation loop tied directly to the completion of passenger trips. When the gravity model confirms a successful route connection between an origin and a destination, the backend engine registers a completed trip. A TypeScript function immediately multiplies this single trip by the current fare price established in the Redux store. The resulting value is dispatched as an incrementing payload to the central treasury variable, ensuring real-time revenue accumulation as the simulation clock advances.

### 5.7.3 Service Hour Purchasing Logic
The conversion of treasury capital into operational capacity requires strict validation logic. When the player attempts to purchase additional service hours through the user interface, the React component dispatches a purchase request. The Redux reducer evaluates the current treasury balance against the fixed $500 cost per service hour. If the funds are sufficient, the system deducts the required capital from the treasury and permanently increments the maximum service hour variable. This immediately makes the new temporal capacity available for route scheduling.

### 5.7.4 Fleet Maintenance and Capital Depreciation
The fleet upkeep mechanic acts as a continuous financial drain that the player must manage actively. The backend engine tracks the accumulation of active service hours across the entire network, gradually increasing a global fleet age variable. As this age variable increases, the mathematical baseline speed of the vehicles decreases, forcing routes to consume more service hours to maintain the same frequency. To counteract this degradation, the player can allocate capital from the treasury to a maintenance function. This action dispatches a reducer that consumes a specific volume of revenue to mathematically lower the fleet age variable, instantly restoring operational network efficiency.

## 6.0 Project Roadmap
### 6.1 Development Milestones
The project will follow a structured, iterative development approach divided into four primary phases. All specific timelines are currently to be determined.
* Phase 1 Minimum Viable Product (MVP): The initial build will focus strictly on the core interactive mechanics. This includes establishing the 100x100 grid canvas, implementing the click and drag route drawing tool, allowing independent bus stop placement, and basic service hour calculations.
* Phase 2 Alpha Build: This phase will introduce the simulation dynamics. The focus will be on programming the autonomous city growth, implementing the dynamic demographic typologies, rendering the colour-coded heatmap, and integrating the half-life spatial decay formula for ridership.
* Phase 3 Beta Build: The Beta phase will layer in the user interface and complex mathematical models. Developers will build the permanent UI dock, the pinnable floating menus, the interactive frequency line graphs, the gravity model for origin-destination logic, and the fare elasticity mechanics.
* Phase 4 Release Candidate: The final phase will involve comprehensive aesthetic polishing to ensure the elegant and minimalist visual standard is met. This phase also includes performance optimization for web browsers and rigorous balancing of the mathematical models to ensure the gameplay remains engaging for a casual audience.

### 6.2 Technical Stack and Build Strategy
To achieve an elegant web-based application, the build will utilize modern web development frameworks and a highly structured implementation sequence. The complexity of the underlying mathematical models requires a robust and scalable architecture.

### 6.2.1 Development Environment and AI Tooling
The foundational build environment will rely on advanced code editors optimized for artificial intelligence integration. Developers will utilize tools such as Cursor or Visual Studio Code paired with Google AI Studio and Google Antigravity. These integrated AI assistants will accelerate the generation of boilerplate code, assist in formatting complex mathematical algorithms, and troubleshoot logic errors during the testing of the gravity model and spatial decay formulas. Standard version control will be maintained using Git and GitHub to track iterative changes across the development phases.

### 6.2.2 Core Programming Languages and Front-End Frameworks
The core game logic and mathematical systems will be written in TypeScript. TypeScript provides strict type checking, which is essential for managing the massive arrays of data generated by a 100x100 grid system without introducing fatal memory errors. The user interface, including the permanent dock, the pinnable floating menus, and the interactive frequency line graphs, will be built utilizing React. React is chosen for its component-based architecture, allowing the complex UI modules to render and update independently over the main game canvas without interrupting the simulation frame rate.

### 6.2.3 Visual Rendering Technologies
Rendering a 100x100 grid with 10,000 individual squares calculating dynamic colour shifts requires highly efficient visual processing. Standard document object model rendering is insufficient for this workload. The project will utilize WebGL via a lightweight 2D rendering engine such as PixiJS. This approach offloads the visual calculations to the user's graphics processing unit, ensuring that the elegant, minimalist aesthetic and the dynamic demographic heatmaps maintain a smooth frame rate even at 10x simulation speed.

### 6.2.4 Data Architecture and State Management
The simulation requires a centralized system to track the status of the grid, the active routes, the available service hours, and the autonomous city growth. The build will implement a state management library such as Redux. This ensures that every component, from the visual heatmap to the financial dashboard, draws from a single source of truth. The primary data structure will involve a master array representing the grid lots, with each object in the array storing localized variables for population density, demographic typologies, and active bus stop catchment modifiers.

### 6.2.5 Sequential Implementation Path
The technical build will follow a strict sequential path to ensure foundational systems are stable before complex logic is introduced.
1. Environment and Canvas: Establish the React framework, initialize the PixiJS WebGL canvas, and generate the baseline 100x100 visual grid.
2. Data Structures: Build the underlying TypeScript arrays to represent the grid lots and programme the basic demographic generation for the starting 10x10 core.
3. Interaction Tools: Implement the user input mechanisms. This includes the click and drag route drawing tool, the vertex snapping logic, and the separate bus stop placement function.
4. Simulation Engine: Programme the core game loop and time manipulation controls. Introduce the autonomous urban expansion algorithm and the dynamic colour-shifting visual feedback.
5. Mathematical Logic: Layer in the complex calculations. Implement the half-life spatial decay formula for bus stops, the gravity model for passenger destination selection, and the network connectivity checks.
6. User Interface: Construct the permanent dock, the route customization popups, and the interactive frequency graphs. Link these UI elements directly to the underlying service hour calculations and revenue generation models.
7. Economy and Polish: Implement the fare elasticity math, test the progression logic for unlocking new service hours, and apply final aesthetic polishing to the user interface.

### 6.3 Testing and Quality Assurance
Testing will remain strictly internal during the initial development cycle. Quality assurance will focus primarily on two areas. The first is validating the mathematical models to ensure the fare elasticity, spatial decay, and route time penalties function correctly together without breaking the game economy. The second is usability testing to verify that the complex data analytics are successfully translated into an intuitive, puzzle-like experience for the player.

### 6.4 Launch Strategy
The current focus is entirely on achieving a stable and functional internal build. External release strategies, public Early Access considerations, and marketing efforts will be deferred until the completion of the Beta phase.
